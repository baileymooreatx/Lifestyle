# Blood Pressure

Blood pressure is measured as two numbers, written as **systolic over diastolic
** (e.g., 120/80 mmHg). These numbers represent the pressure in your arteries
during different phases of the heartbeat cycle.

## The Two Numbers Explained

### Systolic Blood Pressure (The Top Number)

Systolic blood pressure is the **higher** number in a reading. It measures the
force exerted against your artery walls when your heart **contracts** (beats) to
pump oxygen-rich blood out to the rest of your body. This represents the maximum
pressure in your arteries during a heartbeat.

* **Normal Range:** Generally below 120 mmHg.
* **Significance:** As people age, arteries often stiffen, causing systolic
  pressure to rise. High systolic pressure is a major risk factor for heart
  disease and stroke, particularly in individuals over 50.

### Diastolic Blood Pressure (The Bottom Number)

Diastolic blood pressure is the **lower** number. It measures the pressure in
your arteries when your heart **relaxes** between beats to refill with blood.
This represents the minimum pressure in your arteries.

* **Normal Range:** Generally below 80 mmHg.
* **Significance:** While often receiving less attention than systolic pressure,
  elevated diastolic pressure is still dangerous. It indicates that your
  arteries are under constant high pressure, even when the heart is resting.

## Blood Pressure Categories

Healthcare providers use these ranges (based on guidelines from the American
Heart Association) to classify blood pressure health:

| Category                | Systolic (Top Number) |        | Diastolic (Bottom Number) |
|:------------------------|:----------------------|:-------|:--------------------------|
| **Normal**              | Less than 120         | and    | Less than 80              |
| **Elevated**            | 120 – 129             | and    | Less than 80              |
| **High (Stage 1)**      | 130 – 139             | or     | 80 – 89                   |
| **High (Stage 2)**      | 140 or higher         | or     | 90 or higher              |
| **Hypertensive Crisis** | Higher than 180       | and/or | Higher than 120           |

*Note: A diagnosis of hypertension usually requires consistently high readings
over time, not just a single measurement.*

## Which Number Is More Important?

Historically, doctors focused heavily on the diastolic number, but modern
research emphasizes the **systolic** number, especially for adults over 50.

* **Systolic Focus:** Studies show that high systolic pressure is a stronger
  predictor of cardiovascular disease and stroke risk in older adults because it
  reflects the stiffness of large arteries.
* **Diastolic Importance:** The diastolic number remains critical. High
  diastolic pressure (Isolated Diastolic Hypertension) is more common in younger
  adults and still significantly increases the risk of heart attack and stroke.
  For every 10-point increase in diastolic pressure, the risk of death from
  heart disease and stroke can double in middle-aged adults.

Both numbers are essential for a complete picture of heart health.

## Measure Blood Pressure Correctly

To correctly measure your blood pressure at home, follow these evidence-based
steps to ensure accuracy:

### Preparation (30 Minutes Before)

* **Avoid Stimulants:** Do not smoke, drink caffeine (coffee, tea, soda), or
  exercise for at least **30 minutes** prior to measurement, as these can
  temporarily spike your numbers.
* **Empty Your Bladder:** A full bladder can add 10–15 mmHg to your reading.
* **Rest:** Sit quietly in a chair for **5 minutes** before taking the first
  reading. Do not talk, text, or scroll on your phone during this time.

### Proper Positioning

* **Sit Correctly:** Sit in a chair with your **back supported** and **feet flat
  on the floor**. Do not cross your legs.
* **Arm Position:** Rest your arm on a flat surface (like a table) so that the
  cuff is at **heart level**. The cuff should be placed on your **bare upper
  arm**, about 1 inch (2-3 cm) above the elbow crease.
* **Cuff Fit:** Ensure the cuff fits snugly but not too tight. Using a cuff that
  is too small for your arm is a common mistake that leads to falsely high
  readings.

### Taking the Measurement

* **Stay Silent:** Do not talk while the machine is inflating or deflating.
* **Take Multiple Readings:** Take **two readings**, waiting **1 to 2 minutes**
  between them.
* **Record the Average:** Discard the first reading if it is significantly
  different, but generally, average the two numbers for your record. Single
  readings can be misleading due to natural fluctuation.

### When and How Often to Measure

* **Timing:** Measure at the same times each day. The best times are typically:
    * **Morning:** Before eating or taking any medications.
    * **Evening:** Before dinner or bed.
* **Duration:** For a diagnosis or medication adjustment, doctors often
  recommend measuring twice daily for **3 to 7 days** and averaging all the
  results.

### Common Mistakes to Avoid

* **Measuring over clothing:** Always place the cuff on bare skin.
* **Unsupported back or feet:** Slouching or dangling feet can raise readings.
* **Talking or moving:** Even small movements or conversation can alter results.
* **Wrist monitors:** Upper arm monitors are generally more accurate than wrist
  or finger devices. If you must use a wrist monitor, hold it strictly at heart
  level.
