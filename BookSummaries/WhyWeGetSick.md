# _Why We Get Sick_

_The Hidden Epidemic at the Root of Most Chronic Disease - and How to Fight
It_  
by Benjamin Bikman PhD

## Synopsis

*Why We Get Sick* by Benjamin Bikman, PhD, is built around a single central
claim: **insulin resistance is the hidden, unifying root cause of most chronic
diseases** — not just diabetes, but heart disease, Alzheimer's, cancer,
polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), kidney failure, and more. Bikman argues that
over half of U.S. adults (and possibly up to 88%) are already insulin resistant
without knowing it, because conventional medicine focuses on blood glucose
rather than insulin levels.

The book is organized into three parts:

1. **The Problem** — Bikman walks through how chronic hyperinsulinemia damages
   nearly every organ system: it raises blood pressure, drives atherosclerosis,
   impairs brain glucose uptake (contributing to Alzheimer's and Parkinson's),
   disrupts reproductive hormones (PCOS, low testosterone, early puberty),
   promotes tumor growth, accelerates aging, and destroys kidney function. He
   frames metabolic syndrome not as a collection of unrelated criteria but as a
   downstream expression of one upstream problem.
2. **The Causes** — He identifies what drives insulin resistance: a modern diet
   heavy in refined carbohydrates and omega-6 fats, sedentary behavior, poor
   sleep, environmental toxins (BPA, air pollution, pesticides), low-salt diets,
   and the natural decline in insulin sensitivity with age and hormonal shifts.
   Genetics set a baseline but do not determine fate.
3. **The Solution** — Bikman offers an evidence-based lifestyle plan: a
   **low-carbohydrate diet** (targeting ~100 g/day of low-glycemic-load carbs,
   prioritizing protein at 1.0–1.5 g/kg), **intermittent fasting** (18 to 24
   hour windows), regular **exercise** (at least 20 minutes most days, with
   progressive overload), and reduction of omega-6 fatty acids. He argues this
   approach can reverse insulin resistance entirely, whereas drugs and bariatric
   surgery treat symptoms and often fail long-term (~25% of surgical patients
   regain weight and IR).

The book's tone is that of a scientist making a public-health case: "nothing I
write in this book is based on my opinion, but rather published, peer-reviewed
science." It is 280 pages, written for a general audience, and includes
practical food lists, meal suggestions, and an exercise plan in the appendices.

Here's a chapter-by-chapter summary.

## Part I: The Problem — What Is Insulin Resistance and Why Does It Matter?

### Chapter 1: What Is Insulin Resistance

Defines insulin resistance (IR) as a reduced cellular response to insulin,
forcing the pancreas to produce ever more. Bikman argues IR is a far better
marker of type 2 diabetes than blood glucose alone, and that at least half
(possibly up to 88%) of American adults have it. He establishes that insulin
acts on virtually every organ, setting up the book's central thesis: **excess
insulin is a common driver of chronic disease**.

### Chapter 2: Insulin Resistance and Heart Health

Details multiple mechanisms by which IR drives cardiovascular disease: it raises
aldosterone (increasing blood pressure and salt retention), stimulates
endothelial growth, activates the sympathetic nervous system, increases small
dense LDL particles, promotes oxidative stress, and fuels atherosclerotic plaque
formation. Bikman also links IR to both dilated and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy,
and notes that no single factor is more strongly associated with CVD than IR.

### Chapter 3: Insulin Resistance, the Brain, and Neurological Disorders

Connects IR to Alzheimer's disease (second only to the apoE4 gene as a risk
factor), vascular dementia, and Parkinson's. The mechanism is impaired glucose
uptake in the brain leading to energy deficits. He also notes that
dopamine-lowering medications for Parkinson's can temporarily worsen IR.

### Chapter 4: Insulin Resistance and Reproductive Health

Covers IR's effects on pregnancy (gestational diabetes, preeclampsia, oversized
babies), PCOS (IR disrupts estrogen release and androgen conversion, preventing
a dominant follicle from forming), male infertility (lower testosterone, sperm
dysfunction, erectile dysfunction via both hormonal and vascular pathways), and
earlier-onset puberty driven by fat storage and leptin signaling.

### Chapter 5: Insulin Resistance and Cancer

Explains the Warburg effect (cancer cells' reliance on glycolysis) and links IR
to breast, prostate, and colorectal cancers. Breast cancer tissue has ~6× more
insulin receptors than normal; prostate cancer shows similar overexpression.
Colorectal cancer risk is 2–3× higher with IR. Bikman frames this as one
controllable risk factor among many.

### Chapter 6: Insulin and Aging, the Skin, Muscles, and Bones

Discusses how chronic hyperinsulinemia accelerates aging, causes skin changes
(acanthosis nigricans, skin tags), degrades muscle function, and weakens bone
health.

Acanthosis nigricans (AN) is a skin condition characterized by dark, thickened,
velvety patches that typically appear in body folds — most commonly the back of
the neck, armpits, groin, and knuckles.

### Chapter 7: Insulin Resistance and Gastrointestinal and Kidney Health

IR is the strongest predictor of Nonalcoholic Fatty Liver Disease (NAFLD) (15×
higher risk) and a major driver of kidney failure (4× greater risk in the most
insulin-resistant individuals). About 63% of insulin-resistant people experience
gastrointestinal issues.

### Chapter 8: Insulin Resistance, the Metabolic Syndrome, and Obesity

Notes that roughly one-third of adults now meet criteria for metabolic syndrome.
Bikman argues IR is not merely one criterion but the upstream driver of the
others (central obesity, hypertension, dyslipidemia, hyperglycemia). He
criticizes the common practice of giving more insulin to type 2 diabetics,
arguing it worsens the underlying problem.

## Part II: Causes — What Makes Us Insulin Resistant in the First Place?

### Chapter 9: How Age and Genetics Influence Insulin

Reviews the genetic susceptibility to IR (certain gene variants increase risk)
and the natural age-related decline in insulin sensitivity. Emphasizes that
genetics set a baseline but do not determine destiny—lifestyle can modify risk
at every stage.

### Chapter 10: How Hormones Cause Insulin Resistance

Explores how hormonal imbalances (cortisol, growth hormone, sex hormones,
thyroid) both cause and result from IR, creating feedback loops. Covers how
hormonal shifts during aging, menopause, and stress contribute to metabolic
deterioration.

### Chapter 11: Obesity and Insulin Resistance, Revisited

Revisits the obesity–IR relationship, distinguishing between the two. Discusses
lipodystrophy (inability to form new fat cells), ectopic fat deposition, and how
muscles can become insulin-resistant even before fat tissue. Notes that
ceramides are more harmful than triglycerides in driving IR.

### Chapter 12: Inflammation and Oxidative Stress

Describes how chronic inflammation (from visceral fat hypertrophy, autoimmune
conditions, infections) worsens IR, and how reactive oxygen species (ROS) damage
insulin signaling. Visceral fat releases inflammatory mediators that recruit
macrophages, creating a self-amplifying cycle.

### Chapter 13: Lifestyle Factors

Catalogs environmental and behavioral contributors: air pollution (PM 2.5),
tobacco smoke (including secondhand), MSG, petrochemicals (BPA), pesticides,
low-salt diets (raising aldosterone), sedentary behavior (even short periods
increase IR), and sleep deprivation (one week of poor sleep can increase IR by
~30%).

## Part III: The Solution — How Can We Fight Insulin Resistance?

### Chapter 14: Get Moving: The Importance of Physical Activity

Advocates for exercise most days (at least 20 minutes, 6 days/week), emphasizing
full-body, high-intensity movements and progressive overload. Also covers cold
exposure (activates brown fat, increases adiponectin) as an adjunct.

### Chapter 15: Eat Smart: The Evidence on the Food We Eat

The most detailed chapter. Argues the evidence for low-carbohydrate diets is so
strong that the ADA has added it as an option. Key points:

- **Glycemic load** matters more than glycemic index
- Insulin-resistant people do better on low-carb than carb-rich diets (A-Z
  trial)
- **Ketones** are beneficial alternative fuel with anti-inflammatory and
  mitochondrial effects
- **Fasting** (18–24 hours) lowers insulin; eating less frequently is key
- **Protein** raises insulin modestly (~2× fasting) and should be prioritized
  (1.0–1.5 g/kg)
- **Carb target:** up to ~100 g/day, favoring low-glycemic-load foods
- **Omega-6/linoleic acid:** Bikman argues it promotes oxidative stress and
  impairs fat cell proliferation
- LCHF improves triglycerides, blood pressure, testosterone, PCOS, GERD, and may
  help dementia and migraines

### Chapter 16: Conventional Interventions: Drugs and Surgery

Reviews pharmaceutical options (metformin, GLP-1 agonists, statins) and
bariatric surgery. Concludes that ~25% of surgical patients regain weight and
IR, and that drug interventions treat symptoms rather than the root cause.
Lifestyle intervention can often eliminate the underlying problem entirely.

### Chapter 17: The Plan: Putting Research into Action

A practical integration chapter.

- **Tracking:** Fasting insulin < 6 is ideal; 7–17 warrants action. Insulin peak
  timing (30 min = good, 120 min = definite IR).
- **Food rules:** Keep glycemic load < 15 when possible; avoid "sweet"
  condiments; be "starch smart"; don't drink your carbs; use 
  [stevia](../OtherFoods/Stevia.md), erythritol, or monk fruit as sweeteners.
- **Supplements/nutrients:** Cysteine (from methionine) may help prevent IR;
  vitamin D deficiency worsens IR; dairy may reduce IR risk.
- **Meal suggestions** for breakfast, lunch, and dinner following the low-carb,
  high-fat, adequate-protein framework.
- **Exercise plan** (detailed in Appendix A).

## Appendix A: My Sample Daily Exercise Plan

A starter workout that can be tailored over time as you become more
knowledgeable with resistance training and the broad array of exercises. For
each exercise, perform **two to four sets**, going to failure (or very nearly)
on each set, usually at around **eight to 20 repetitions**. If the names of
certain exercises are unfamiliar, a simple internet search will reveal the
motion. Also, for the sake of simplicity, I've included some "calisthenic"
options—exercises that can be performed with only body weight as the resistance.

### Monday: Puller Leg Day

This is a day that will strengthen, among other things, the back of your body,
including your lower back, buttocks, and hamstrings. After focusing on the big
puller muscles, finish with a focus on calves.

| Weight Training          | Calisthenics                       |
|--------------------------|------------------------------------|
| 1. Deadlift              | 1. Bridge                          |
| 2. Straight-leg deadlift | 2. One-legged body-weight deadlift |
| 3. Reverse lunge         | 3. Natural hamstring curls         |

**Finish with calves:**

| Weight Training               | Calisthenics         |
|-------------------------------|----------------------|
| 1. Straight leg calf press    | 2. Seated calf raise |
| 1. Standing static calf press |                      |

### Tuesday: Pusher-Upper Day

This day focuses on the chest and shoulders. Always start with the chest, then
proceed to shoulders—all chest exercises require the involvement of the
shoulders, and if you start with shoulders, they will be your weak point and
your chest will be left "undone."

**Chest:**

| Weight Training                                    | Calisthenics                                |
|----------------------------------------------------|---------------------------------------------|
| 1. Dumbbell bench press                            | 1. Pseudo planche push-up                   |
| 2. Dumbbell fly                                    | 2. Pseudo planche                           |
| 3. Push-ups                                        | 3. Wall handstand (with or without push-up) |
| 4. Wide-grip push-up (with or without static hold) |                                             |

**Shoulders (finish):**

1. Standing one-arm dumbbell shoulder press
2. Standing barbell shoulder press
3. Arnold press

### Wednesday: Aerobic and Stomach Day

This day allows for general recovery, while performing, insofar as possible, a *
*20-minute high-intensity interval aerobic session** (e.g., interval running or
cycling) with a series of stomach exercises at the end.

> **Note about the stomach:** Go slow. Avoid the temptation to speedily complete
> the motions; rather, perform the motion slowly, contracting the stomach hard
> throughout the entire motion. Don't relax your stomach at any point—it helps to
> never let any space come between your lower back and the bench/ground. Also, at
> peak contraction, exhale powerfully. As usual, go to failure, though it may
> take ~20 repetitions.

**Stomach exercises (Weight Training / Calisthenics):**

1. Knee crunches
2. Leg lifts
3. Heel touches

*Remember—go slowly with stomach exercises; form is more important than total
repetitions.*

### Thursday: Pusher Leg Day

This is an important day, strengthening the muscles that allow you to move
around, including running and even simply getting up from the ground or a seated
position.

| Weight Training | Calisthenics                                      |
|-----------------|---------------------------------------------------|
| 1. Squats       | 1. Pistol squat (with or without wall assistance) |
| 2. Split squats | 2. Box jumps                                      |
| 3. Lunges       | 3. Single-leg walking lunge                       |
| 4. Step-ups     | 4. Standing static calf press                     |

**Finish with calves (as with puller leg day):**

1. Straight leg calf press
2. Seated calf raise

### Friday: Puller-Upper Day

These exercises target your back, which involves a pulling movement in two
ways—pulling with arms above the head, so your hands move toward your shoulders,
and pulling with arms in front, so your hands move toward your body.

| Weight Training                                                           | Calisthenics                                                                                  |
|---------------------------------------------------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| 1. Pull-ups (work up to real pull-ups by using a weight-assisted machine) | 1. Any variation of pull-ups (wide grip, narrow grip, archer, etc.; keep chest up throughout) |
| 2. Bent-over barbell row                                                  | 2. Front lever (start with knees bent and progress toward straight legs)                      |
| 3. Lat pulldown                                                           |                                                                                               |
| 4. One-arm dumbbell row                                                   |                                                                                               |

### Saturday: Aerobic and Stomach Day

*Repeat of Wednesday*

*Not explicitly stated, but implied by the 6-day structure*
