# Seed Oils

The claim that seed oils are "bad for you" is popular on social media but **not
strongly supported by the evidence**. Here's what critics argue and what the
research actually shows.

**The main arguments against seed oils:**

- **Omega-6 / inflammation:** Seed oils (canola, soybean, sunflower, etc.) are
  high in linoleic acid (LA), an omega-6 fat. Critics say it's converted to
  arachidonic acid, which promotes inflammation. In reality, only a tiny
  fraction is converted, and large human studies show **no increase in
  inflammation** — some even show a reduction.
- **Conflation with ultra-processed foods:** Seed oils are ubiquitous in chips,
  fast food, and packaged meals. The health harms people attribute to the oil
  are almost certainly from the **sugar, sodium, additives, and excess
  calories** in those foods, not the oil itself.
- **Oxidation at high heat:** Repeatedly heating seed oils (as in deep-frying)
  can produce aldehydes and trans fats. This is a real concern for restaurant
  fryer oil, but it applies to any oil reused past its smoke point.
- **Chemical processing:** Extraction with hexane and refining strips some
  antioxidants. Residual hexane in finished products is negligible and regulated
  to safe levels.

**What the evidence says:**

A 2025 *American Medical Association* study of three large cohorts (33 years of
data) found that higher intake of plant-based oils (canola, soybean, olive)
was associated with **lower total mortality**, while higher butter intake was
linked to higher risk. Harvard, Stanford, Johns Hopkins, and the American Heart
Association all conclude that seed oils are **not inherently harmful** and may
be heart-healthy when used in place of saturated fats.

The real dietary concern is overconsumption of ultra-processed foods that happen
to contain seed oils rather than the oils themselves.

Here's a deeper dive into each of the main claims and what the evidence actually
shows.

## The Inflammation Claim

The core argument is: seed oils are high in **linoleic acid (LA)** → LA converts
to **arachidonic acid (AA)** → AA produces pro-inflammatory eicosanoids (
prostaglandins, leukotrienes) → chronic inflammation → disease.

The problem with this chain is the second step. The conversion of LA to AA in
the human body is **extremely limited** (roughly 1–2% per pass through the
delta-6 desaturase enzyme), and multiple Randomized Controlled Trials (RCTs)  
have shown that increasing dietary LA does **not** meaningfully raise 
circulating AA levels. A 2012 systematic review of RCTs in healthy adults 
concluded: *"virtually no evidence is available from randomized, controlled 
intervention studies among healthy, noninfant human beings to show that addition 
of LA to the diet increases the concentration of inflammatory markers"* 
(CRP, IL-6, TNF-α, fibrinogen, etc.).

More recent data goes further in the **opposite** direction:

- A **Framingham Offspring Study** analysis of 2,022 adults found red blood cell
  LA was **inversely** associated with IL-6 and CRP.
- A **2025 Nutrition 2025** study (Maki et al., ~1,900 participants) found
  higher plasma LA was associated with **lower** CRP, glycoprotein acetyls,
  serum amyloid A, glucose, insulin, and HOMA-IR (insulin resistance).
- A 2024 meta-analysis by Jackson et al. found higher LA intake consistently
  correlated with **lower** CRP and IL-6.

So the inflammation narrative is built on a biochemical pathway that doesn't
play out in humans at dietary levels.

## The Omega-6 : Omega-3 Ratio

A popular claim is that the modern diet's omega-6:omega-3 ratio (often cited as
15:1 to 20:1, vs. an "ancestral" 1:1) is the problem. In reality:

- The "ancestral 1:1 ratio" is **not well-established** — estimates from
  hunter-gatherer populations vary widely (roughly 2:1 to 4:1 depending on the
  group studied).
- A WHO study found that a higher omega-3:6 ratio was linked to a 26% reduced
  risk of depression, but concluded that **high omega-6 intake from seed oils is
  unlikely to increase risk of death or disease**.
- The practical takeaway from experts is to **increase omega-3 intake** (fatty
  fish, walnuts, flaxseed) rather than eliminate omega-6s.

## Genetic Variability (a nuance worth noting)

A 2019 study found that people with different variants of the **FADS1** gene
respond differently to LA. Some variants showed reduced blood sugar and
inflammation with LA supplementation; others showed the opposite. This is an
active area of research and could eventually explain why a small subset of
people *might* be more susceptible to inflammatory effects from high omega-6
intake. But this is far from being a basis for a blanket "seed oils are bad"
recommendation.

## The Ultra-Processed Food Confound (the real issue)

This is where the "seed oils are bad" narrative gets its most plausible-sounding
support, and where the scientific consensus is strongest:

- Seed oils are the **dominant cooking and processing fat** in the US food
  supply. They're in chips, crackers, fast food, baked goods, frozen meals, and
  most packaged snacks.
- Diets high in **ultra-processed foods (UPFs)** are robustly associated with
  cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, obesity, and earlier mortality. A
  2026 study of 8.8 million people linked UPF consumption to early death.
- The health harms people observe are almost certainly driven by the **sugar,
  sodium, refined starches, excess calories, and lack of fiber** in those
  foods — not the oil itself.
- Stanford's Gardner puts it plainly: *"The real concern should be overeating
  ultra-processed foods, which may contain harmful ingredients such as
  high-fructose corn syrup, added sugar and sodium."*

In other words, seed oils are a **proxy** for UPF consumption in the data, which
creates a spurious association.

## Cholesterol and Cardiovascular Evidence

This is where the evidence is most unambiguous:

- **RCTs consistently show** that replacing saturated fat (butter, lard, tallow)
  with polyunsaturated seed oils **lowers LDL cholesterol** and cardiovascular
  risk.
- A 2025 cohort of ~200,000 people followed for ~33 years found the highest
  intake of plant-based oils (canola, soybean, olive) was associated with **16%
  lower total mortality**, **11% lower cancer mortality**, and **6% lower
  cardiovascular mortality**. The same cohort found the highest butter intake
  was associated with **15% higher total mortality**.
- Replacing butter with plant-based oils was associated with a **17% reduction
  in total mortality**.
- A 2025 *Journal of Nutrition* review concluded: *"there is no evidence that
  linoleic acid is linked to higher inflammation"* and that higher LA intake is
  either neutral or inversely linked to inflammatory markers.

## Oxidation and Processing Concerns

Two legitimate but often overstated points:

1. **Oxidation at high heat:** Repeatedly heating any polyunsaturated oil past
   its smoke point (as in commercial deep-frying) produces aldehydes and other
   oxidation products. This is a real concern for restaurant fryer oil, but it's
   a problem of **oil reuse and temperature management**, not of the oil itself.
   Home cooking at moderate temperatures doesn't produce meaningful levels of
   these compounds.

2. **Industrial processing (hexane extraction, deodorization):** These steps do
   remove some natural antioxidants (vitamin E, phytosterols). Residual hexane
   in finished oil is regulated to <10 ppm and is considered negligible. The
   2026 scoping review in *Critical Reviews in Food Science and Nutrition*
   concluded that concerns about processing byproducts (3-MCPD esters, glycidyl
   esters) are **"without scientific foundation"** at typical dietary exposure
   levels.

## The Dissenting View

A 2026 narrative synthesis in *PubMed Central* argued that the **temporal
correlation** between the adoption of LA-rich seed oils in the early 20th
century and the rise in coronary heart disease mortality supports the hypothesis
that LA may have *contributed* to the coronary heart disease (CHD) epidemic.  
They applied Bradford Hill criteria and suggested further investigation. However, 
this is a **hypothesis-generating** analysis based on ecological 
(population-level) data, not a causal demonstration. Multiple confounders 
(smoking, urbanization, physical inactivity, rise in trans fats) changed 
simultaneously during that period. No RCT or well-controlled cohort has 
confirmed a causal role for dietary LA in increasing CHD risk.

## American Heart Association: Conflict of Interest

Allegations that the American Heart Association (AHA) received money from seed
oil companies to endorse their products is one of the most repeated claims in
the anti-seed-oil narrative, and the reality is **more nuanced than either side
presents**.

### What actually happened in 1948

The claim goes: "Procter & Gamble paid the AHA \$1.7 million (≈$20M today) to 
promote vegetable oils." The AHA has **directly disputed this**. On its own 
website, the AHA states:

> "People across the country donated \$1.5 million in 1948 to the American 
> Heart Association as part of a radio show contest. Procter & Gamble did not 
> make the \$1.5 million donation to the American Heart Association. The 
> company sponsored
> the radio show, and its Duz laundry soap and Ivory soap were promoted by the
> host throughout the show but separately from the contest."

So the money came from **radio listeners** on P&G's *Truth or Consequences*
show, not from P&G's corporate treasury. P&G designated the AHA as the contest's
beneficiary and promoted its soaps on the show. Crisco (a vegetable shortening)
was **not** the product being promoted in the contest.

### What's true about the conflict of interest

Even granting the AHA's version, there are legitimate concerns.

- **P&G sponsored the show** that made the AHA a national organization. The
  AHA's own history describes this as the "bang of big bucks" that "launched"
  the group. P&G had a clear strategic interest in a prominent heart-health
  organization existing.
- **P&G continued to support AHA activities** for decades after, and the AHA has
  received ongoing support from food industry groups. In 2017, *LA Times*
  columnist Nina Teicholz noted that **Bayer** (owner of LibertyLink soybeans)
  pledged up to $500,000 to the AHA.
- The AHA's 1961 dietary guidelines, which recommended replacing saturated fat
  with vegetable oils, came shortly after **Ancel Keys** joined the AHA
  Nutrition Committee. Keys was a vocal advocate of the diet-heart hypothesis
  and had connections to the vegetable oil industry.

### What's true about industry-funded research

This is the strongest version of the argument:

- Multiple seed oil industry groups — **Soy Nutrition Institute (SNI) Global**,
  **United Soybean Board**, **National Corn Growers Association**, **Canola
  Council of Canada**, **U.S. Canola Association** — fund peer-reviewed research
  on seed oil health benefits.
- A 2022 study in *Public Health Nutrition* found that **95% of the 2020–2025
  U.S. Dietary Guidelines committee members** had conflicts of interest with
  food and/or pharmaceutical industries.
- The pattern mirrors what's been documented for sugar and soda: industry-funded
  studies are more likely to produce favorable results.

However, **SNI Global itself** (an industry group) has published a fact sheet
arguing that the key RCTs and cohort studies behind AHA recommendations were
funded by **government and academic sources**, not industry. Whether you trust
that assessment depends on whether you trust an industry group to audit its own
funding.

### The Balanced Assessment

| **Claim**                                         | **Verdict**                                                                                                                                                                                                                           |
|:--------------------------------------------------|:--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| "P&G directly paid the AHA $1.7M"                 | **False** — the money came from radio listeners; P&G sponsored the show                                                                                                                                                               |
| "P&G had a conflict of interest"                  | **True** — P&G's products were promoted on the same show, and P&G continued funding the AHA                                                                                                                                           |
| "Seed oil industry groups fund research"          | **True** — multiple industry bodies fund peer-reviewed studies                                                                                                                                                                        |
| "The AHA was 'bought' and its science is invalid" | **Overstated** — the core RCT evidence for Polyunsaturated Fatty Acid (PUFA) being better than Saturated Fatty Acids (SFA) for LDL comes from government-funded trials; industry funding is a concern but doesn't invalidate the data |
| "The AHA has a structural conflict of interest"   | **Fair to raise** — ongoing industry funding of a body that sets dietary policy is a legitimate governance concern, even if it doesn't prove the recommendations are wrong                                                            |

The short version: the "P&G bought the AHA" story is **a myth in its strongest
form** but **points at a real and documented pattern** of industry influence on
nutrition science. The AHA's specific scientific conclusions (PUFAs lower LDL,
replacing SFA with PUFA reduces CHD risk) are supported by a body of evidence
that is largely independent of seed oil industry funding. The conflict of
interest is a valid governance criticism, but it doesn't automatically
invalidate the findings.

## Summary

- **You don't need to avoid seed oils.** They're a source of essential fatty
  acids and, in RCTs, improve cholesterol profiles compared to saturated fats.
- **The real dietary lever** is reducing ultra-processed food intake and
  increasing whole foods (vegetables, fruits, legumes, whole grains, fish).
- **If you want to optimize**, focus on increasing omega-3 intake (fatty fish
  2×/week, walnuts, flaxseed) rather than cutting omega-6s.
- **For cooking at home**, canola, soybean, and sunflower oils are fine at
  normal stovetop temperatures. If you're deep-frying, use the oil fresh and
  don't reuse it excessively.
