# Probiotic Fermented Dairy  

These are like very thick yogurt, but they do not contain the microbes species
required to be called yogurt. Instead, they contain microbes that kill harmful
bacteria in the small intestine and promote gut health.

Bacteria Species:  
- _Lactobacillis reuteri_
- _Lactobacillis gasseri_

Per the FDA standard of identity (21 CFR § 131.200), a product can only be
labeled "yogurt" if it is cultured with **both** of these two lactic
acid-producing bacteria:

| **Required Bacteria**                           | **Role**                                                                     |
|:------------------------------------------------|:-----------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| *Lactobacillus delbrueckii* subsp. *bulgaricus* | Produces lactic acid and flavor compounds (acetaldehyde, diacetyl)           |
| *Streptococcus thermophilus*                    | Produces lactic acid and helps *L. bulgaricus* grow (symbiotic relationship) |

**Key regulatory details:**

- **Both must be present** — a product fermented with only one (or with a
  different strain like *L. reuteri* or *L. gasseri*) **cannot** legally be
  called "yogurt."
- The fermentation must reach a **pH of 4.6 or lower** (≥0.7% titratable
  acidity) solely through bacterial action — no added acidulants.
- **Optional additional cultures** are permitted (e.g., *L. acidophilus*,
  *Bifidobacterium* spp., *L. casei*), but they don't substitute for the two
  required ones.
- To claim **"contains live and active cultures,"** the product must have ≥10⁷
  CFU/g at manufacture with a reasonable expectation of ≥10⁶ CFU/g through shelf
  life.

So ferments using *L. reuteri* or *L. gasseri* are a **cultured/fermented dairy
product**, but they could not carry the "yogurt" label because they lack the two
mandatory starter species.

### Equipment

[Instant Pot](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uyG0K1Io5oM)  
Glass or ceramic bowl or other vessel large enough to hold at least one quart of
liquid. I use round 
[Pyrex 7 cup bowls](https://www.amazon.com/Pyrex-Container-Non-Toxic-Dishwasher-Microwave/dp/B0BSJZ1QYZ/) 
with [silicone slings](https://www.amazon.com/Silicone-Handles-Cooling-Bakeware-Roasting/dp/B0DSZYWL1F/) 
to make it easier to lower them into the Instant Pot. They fit perfectly.  

### Ingredients

Starter: 2 tablespoons previous batch of L. reuteri yogurt (whey or curds or
mixture of both)  
1 quart of organic **ulta-pasteurized** half-and-half  

**Optional thickeners:**
½ cup of non-fat powdered milk (more effective) 
1 tablespoon of inulin (less effective)  

The powdered milk will make the yogurt thicker, but it must be heated added 
to the half-and-half while it is cold. Then the mixture needs to be 
pasteurized. The instant pot has a "Boil" setting to do this. Switch to this 
mode by pressing the Yogurt button until the display says "boil." After the 
pasteurization is complete, wait for the milk to cool to 105 °F prevent killing 
the bacteria.  

Mix the dairy with the starter and ferment in the Instant Pot for 36 hours 
using the "Yogurt" function set to "Less/Low."

### Benefits

**Lactobacillus reuteri** (now reclassified as
*[Limosilactobacillus reuteri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limosilactobacillus_reuteri)*)
is a well-studied probiotic strain. Here are its main evidence-backed benefits.

| **Benefit**                                                                                 | **Evidence Strength** |
|:--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|:----------------------|
| **Reduces infant colic** (crying time ↓ ~43 min/day in breastfed infants, strain DSM 17938) | Strongest             |
| **Shortens acute infectious diarrhea** in children (~0.87 days)                             | Moderate              |
| **Reduces *H. pylori* load** as adjunct to standard therapy                                 | Moderate              |
| **Lowers LDL cholesterol** (~12% LDL reduction, strain NCIMB 30242)                         | Moderate              |
| **Improves oral health** (reduces *Streptococcus mutans*, plaque, gingivitis)               | Moderate              |
| **Strengthens gut barrier** / reduces intestinal permeability                               | Moderate              |
| **Immune modulation** (↑ IL-10, ↑ regulatory T cells, ↓ pro-inflammatory cytokines)         | Moderate              |
| **Prevents antibiotic-associated diarrhea**                                                 | Moderate              |
| **Reduces atopic dermatitis / eczema** in infants                                           | Low–Moderate          |
| **Increases vitamin D levels** (~25.5% over 9 weeks)                                        | Emerging              |
| **Protects preterm infants** from necrotizing enterocolitis (risk ↓ 42%, death ↓ 65%)       | Moderate              |

**Key mechanisms:** It produces **reuterin**, an antimicrobial compound that
inhibits pathogenic bacteria, yeasts, and fungi without harming beneficial gut
microbes. It also adheres to intestinal epithelium, stimulates mucus production,
and modulates immune signaling.

**Important caveats:**

- Effects are **strain-dependent** — DSM 17938 is best studied for colic; NCIMB
  30242 for cholesterol.
- Many eye-catching claims (oxytocin, wound healing, weight loss) come from
  **animal studies** without human replication.
- Bone density benefits shown in one small trial were **not confirmed** in a
  larger follow-up.
- Side effects are generally mild: transient gas (~5–15%), abdominal discomfort,
  or mild diarrhea.

## Lactobacillis gasseri
Starter microbes [Swanson Lactobacillus Gasseri](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00OLQLS64)  
Ferment using the same instructions listed above.

### Benefits

Lactobacillus gasseri is one of the most studied probiotic species, with
evidence concentrated in a handful of well-characterized strains. Here's what
the data supports.

| **Benefit**                                                                | **Key Strains**           | **Evidence Strength**            |
|:---------------------------------------------------------------------------|:--------------------------|:---------------------------------|
| **Reduces visceral (abdominal) fat** (4.6–8.5% over 12 weeks)              | SBT2055, BNR17            | Strongest                        |
| **Improves sleep quality** (PSQI score ↓ ~0.77)                            | CP2305                    | Moderate (meta-analysis, 7 RCTs) |
| **Reduces IBS symptoms** (abdominal pain ↓ ~54% at 4 weeks)                | CP2305, LA806             | Moderate                         |
| **Supports vaginal microbiome** / BV recovery                              | TM13 + *L. crispatus*     | Moderate                         |
| **Reduces *H. pylori* load** (adjunct)                                     | —                         | Moderate                         |
| **Improves functional dyspepsia** (epigastric pain, postprandial fullness) | —                         | Moderate                         |
| **Improves bowel function** (frequency, stool consistency)                 | CP2305 (heat-inactivated) | Emerging                         |
| **Modulates metabolic hormones** (↓ leptin, ↑ GLP-1, ↑ PYY)                | LG08                      | Emerging (2026 RCT)              |
| **Reduces fasting glucose** / modulates gut inflammation                   | —                         | Animal + early human             |

**The visceral fat claim in detail:**
The flagship trial (Kadooka et al., 2010; n=87) showed a **4.6% reduction in
visceral fat area** and 1.4% body weight loss over 12 weeks with SBT2055. A
larger follow-up (n=210) confirmed an **8.5% visceral fat reduction** at lower
doses. A separate BNR17 trial (n=90, Korean adults) showed a **21.6 cm²
reduction in visceral fat area**. Critically:

- **Effects reversed within 4 weeks** of stopping the supplement.
- Absolute weight loss was modest: **~1–2 kg (2–4 lbs)** over 12 weeks.
- Most trials were conducted in **Japanese or Korean populations** with elevated
  visceral fat.
- One trial (n=62) found **no significant effect**, so results are not perfectly
  consistent.

**Sleep and stress (CP2305):**
A 2023 meta-analysis of 7 RCTs found that daily CP2305 significantly improved
sleep quality in adults with mild-to-moderate stress. A 24-week trial in medical
students showed reduced anxiety (Spielberger) and improved PSQI scores. A 2025
study also showed heat-inactivated CP2305 improved bowel function, suggesting
some effects may not require live bacteria.

**Key caveats:**

- **Strain-specificity matters enormously.** SBT2055 and BNR17 are for fat;
  CP2305 is for sleep/IBS; LG08 for metabolic hormones. You can't generalize
  across strains.
- **No authorized health claims** exist in the EU or UK for *L. gasseri* and
  weight loss.
- The evidence base, while solid for a probiotic, is still **limited in scale
  and population diversity**.
- Typical studied doses: **1–10 billion CFU/day** for 12+ weeks.

## Fermentation

Both strains share the same optimal window, 36 hours at 97–100 °F, but they
differ in *when* they actually start proliferating, which is the critical 
detail.  

|                                          | *L. gasseri*                     | *L. reuteri*                     |
|:-----------------------------------------|:---------------------------------|:---------------------------------|
| **Optimal ferment time**                 | **36 h**                         | **36 h**                         |
| **Temperature**                          | 97–100 °F (36–38 °C)             | 97–100 °F (36–38 °C)             |
| **Lag phase** (before meaningful growth) | **~24 h**                        | **~30 h**                        |
| **Peak count**                           | End of 36 h                      | End of 36 h                      |
| **What happens at 24 h**                 | Just *starting* to proliferate   | Still in lag; minimal growth     |
| **What happens at 36 h**                 | Full exponential growth achieved | Full exponential growth achieved |

A 24-hour *L. gasseri* ferment is actually **right at the edge of its lag
phase**. It would be pulling it out just as the bacteria *begin* to multiply,
so you'd get far fewer live cultures and less lactose consumed than the 36-hour
version. Both manufacturers (Cutting Edge Cultures, Cultured Food Life) are
emphatic: **do not stop before 36 hours** for either strain.

If we want the full probiotic benefit from either strain, **36 hours is
non-negotiable**. Shorter times sacrifice the exponential growth phase that
gives these ferments their therapeutic value.

## L. gasseri vs. L. reuteri  

They overlap in several areas but have distinct strengths:

| **Area**                 | *L. gasseri*                                                                                                                        | *L. reuteri*                                                                                                               |
|:-------------------------|:------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|:---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Immune profile**       | Pro-Th1 (activates cell-mediated immunity)                                                                                          | More anti-inflammatory (↑ IL-10, ↓ Th2/IgE)                                                                                |
| **Gut barrier**          | Restores tight-junction proteins & goblet cells (mouse colitis model)                                                               | Reduces intestinal permeability ("leaky gut"); strongest barrier-repair evidence of any *Lactobacillus*                    |
| **SCFA / butyrate**      | Modest SCFA contribution                                                                                                            | **Specifically shifts microbiota toward butyrate production** — unique among the two                                       |
| **Weight / metabolic**   | **Strongest evidence**: [visceral fat](../../Body/VisceralFat.md) reduction (BNR17, SBT2055), insulin sensitivity, lipid metabolism | Weaker; some weight/obesity data but less robust                                                                           |
| **Stress / gut-brain**   | **Strongest evidence**: CP2305 reduces stress markers, improves sleep, preserves *Bifidobacterium* under chronic stress             | Some gut-brain data but less specific                                                                                      |
| **Unique antimicrobial** | Gassericin A (cyclic bacteriocin)                                                                                                   | **Reuterin** (3-hydroxypropionaldehyde) — broad-spectrum antimicrobial                                                     |
| **Allergy / asthma**     | Limited                                                                                                                             | **More effective**: reduces Th2 cytokines, total IgE, airway inflammation                                                  |
| **H. pylori**            | Adjunct to eradication therapy                                                                                                      | Adjunct; also improves gastrin-17 / mucosal inflammation                                                                   |
| **Growth in milk**       | Slower; needs peptides (not whole proteins)                                                                                         | Faster; thrives on lactose and glycerol                                                                                    |
| **Long-term / aging**    | Less studied                                                                                                                        | 1-year trial in elderly women: preserved microbiota diversity, improved inflammatory markers, correlated with bone density |

*L. gasseri* leans toward **metabolic/weight benefits, stress resilience, and
upper-GI (gastric) protection**, while *L. reuteri* leans toward **barrier
repair, SCFA/butyrate production, anti-allergic effects, and long-term
microbiome maintenance**. They're complementary rather than redundant, which is
why many fermented-milk protocols use both together.

## Thicker Yogurt

For a thicker result, the levers that actually work are:

- **Higher fat**: We're already at ~18% with half-and-half, about as good as
  it gets without going to cream.
- **Protein denaturation**: Ultra-pasteurized half-and-half already provides
  this. If you switch to milk that is not ultra-pasteurized, heat it to
  180–195 °F for 10 to 20 min first with the Instant Pot Yogurt "Boil" mode.
  Let it cool 105 °F before inoculating it with the starter.
- **Longer fermentation / more generations**: Re-inoculating with a prior batch
  consistently yields a thicker, more uniform curd.
- **Straining whey** for a Greek-style finish

### Increased Protein

Adding non-fat milk powder is one of the most effective levers for curd
structure.

#### What It Does to the Gel

Milk powder increases your **milk solids nonfat (MSNF)**, which means more
casein micelles and (if the whey proteins are denatured) more cross-linking
partners.
A [K-State study](https://krex.k-state.edu/items/ff32cee1-b25c-4f71-a690-f99775c13e5d)
found that higher MSNF concentration directly translates to "a denser protein
gel network, more resistant to compression and shear," with the lowest
syneresis (whey separation) at ~12% MSNF.

#### Structural Trade-off

The microstructure becomes **coarser**, not finer.
A [review of dairy powder fortification](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/258250605_Effect_of_dairy_powders_fortification_on_yogurt_textural_and_sensorial_properties_a_review)
notes that SMP-fortified yogurts show "largest casein micelles and extensive
micelle clusters" with an "irregular gel organization." So you gain firmness and
water-holding at the cost of a slightly grainier, less silky texture.

#### For Ultra-pasteurized Half-and-half

- **Use nonfat dry milk** because we already have 18% fat; we want the protein,
  not more fat.
- **Dissolve it into the cold half-and-half before any heating.** Adding it to
  hot milk causes stringy coagulation. See 
  [OSU Extension](https://extension.oregonstate.edu/sites/extd8/files/documents/8836/fs173emakingyogurt.pdf).
- **The cold-start caveat:** The ultra-pasteurized half-and-half is already 
  denatured, but the *powder's* whey proteins (~20% of its protein) are **not**. 
  Without a heat step, only the casein (~80%) contributes to cross-linking. A
  Reddit user on r/yogurtmaking put it well: "the whey proteins in the added 
  milk powder won't do anything to thicken the yogurt" without heat 
  treatment. If you want the full benefit, heat the reconstituted mixture to
  ~180 °F for 10–20 min before inoculating. This denatures the powder's whey
  proteins so they can bind to casein and strengthen the network.
- **Ratio:** ~⅓ cup (≈80 g) per quart of half-and-half is a good starting point.
  You can push higher for a Skyr-like result.

Result: thicker, firmer, with less whey — but slightly coarser microstructure. 
If you value the ultra-smooth mouthfeel of your current batches, start with a
modest amount and dial up from there.

### Inulin Powder

**The "prebiotic food" claim is largely unsupported for these strains.**

The standard pitch is that [inulin](../../Supplements/Inulin.md) feeds *L. reuteri* 
and *L. gasseri*, boosting growth and CFU counts. But a PLOS study found that 
*L. reuteri* DSM 17938 and ATCC PTA 6475 **cannot ferment inulin-type fructans 
as a carbon source**. The r/ReuteriYogurt community has converged on the same 
conclusion: "Studies have shown that *L. reuteri* does not effectively utilize 
inulin-type fructans as a growth substrate." *L. gasseri* is in a similar 
metabolic category. It's not a fructan fermenter.

So the "extra food for the bacteria" rationale doesn't hold up for these 
specific strains. As one user put it: "you have roughly three times more lactose 
than you need… inulin is not necessary for the growth of 6475."

**What inulin actually does in your system is physical, not biological:**

- **Water retention** —
  a [2023 gel-properties study](https://doi.org/10.3390/gels9110863) found
  inulin had "the most prominent ability" among tested prebiotics to retain
  water in the yogurt matrix, due to its microcrystalline aggregate structure
- **Mild network reinforcement** — inulin forms hydrogen-bonded complexes with
  casein, creating secondary gel structures that reduce syneresis. One study
  found it "strengthened the gel network… provided a more refined, compact
  structure"
- **Modest thickening** — a
  [rheology study on *L.reuteri* yogurt specifically](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/348335471) 
  found inulin concentration had "the greatest influence on G' and flow point"
  among tested variables — but also noted it had a **softening effect** overall,
  which is somewhat contradictory and suggests the effect is subtle and
  system-dependent

**Practical bottom line for your setup:**

We're already at ~18% fat in the half-and-half, which is the dominant factor in
mouthfeel and body. At 1 tablespoon per quart, inulin will give you a **slight**
improvement in water-holding and a marginally denser gel, but it's not going to
be transformative the way milk powder would be. It's a minor textural tweak, not
a structural upgrade.

Dr. Davis's troubleshooting page claims "omitting this step will yield a thinner
end-product with lower bacterial counts," but the community and the literature
don't support the bacterial-count part for your strains. If you're already
getting a thick, uniform curd, the inulin is doing very little. If you're
getting excess whey separation, it might help a small amount — but guar gum (2
tsp per quart) is a more direct fix for that.

### Increased Lactose

Adding extra lactose will **not** make your yogurt thicker. The thickness in
your ferment comes from **protein coagulation**. As the bacteria convert
lactose to lactic acid, the pH drops and casein proteins form a gel network.
That process is **limited by pH, not by sugar availability**. The half-and-half 
already contains roughly three times more lactose than the cultures can consume
before the pH hits its endpoint (~4.0 to 4.2), at which point growth stops
regardless of remaining sugar.

Community consensus (r/ReuteriYogurt) is explicit: "you do NOT want to add any
additional sugar into your milk… the bacteria is limited by the pH, not by the
sugar content."

## New Starter Culture?

Cutting Edge Cultures (the maker of both the LR and LG Superfood starters)
frames it simply: "You can keep reculturing for as long as your batch comes out
thick and pleasantly tangy." For *L. gasseri* specifically, Nourish Me Organics
suggests a slightly more conservative **6 or 7 generations** before quality 
tends to dip, likely because *L. gasseri* is a slower grower and more sensitive 
to the nitrogen demands of milk.

For a single-strain culture, the generational method can theoretically
continue indefinitely. Bob Niland (Dr. Davis's blog associate) states this
directly: "Theoretically, the generational method works indefinitely—for a
single-strain yogurt, and if contamination is avoided. Dr. Davis might have made
100 generations this way." Dr. Davis himself reports being "on my 60th or so
batch and have never reinoculated yet all the benefits are maintained."  

### Contamination  

The only real risk is **contamination**, an unwanted organism hitching a ride
and establishing itself in your chain. There's no inherent "expiration" on a
single strain propagating through its own descendants.

### Reset  

Practical signals that it's time to reset:

- The batch comes out noticeably less tangy or thinner than your norm
- Excess whey separation that wasn't there before
- Off-flavors or off-smells

You don't need a fixed generation count. Run each strain's chain as long as the 
output stays consistent, and reset from a fresh capsule or sachet when you 
notice decline. The frozen-starter backup is still wise as insurance against 
a bad batch, but it's a safety net, not a schedule.
