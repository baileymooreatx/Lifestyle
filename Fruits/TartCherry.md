# Tart Cherries

Tart cherries (*Prunus cerasus*, especially the **Montmorency** variety) support
sleep. A 2012 randomized controlled trial found that drinking Montmorency tart 
cherry juice for 7 days increased melatonin levels and improved both sleep 
duration and efficiency compared to placebo. The effect is thought to be more 
gradual and sustained than a melatonin supplement because it supports the  
body's own production rather than delivering a single large dose.

- **Low-calorie, carb-dominant:** ~92% of calories come from carbohydrates,
  almost entirely natural sugars (glucose and fructose).
- **Higher in vitamin C** than sweet cherry varieties (~16 mg per cup vs. ~11
  mg).
- **Rich in anthocyanins** (the pigment that gives them their deep red color) —
  tart cherries have the highest anthocyanin concentration of any common cherry,
  which drives their anti-inflammatory effects.
- **Contains tryptophan, melatonin, and procyanidin B-2** in small but
  biologically meaningful amounts.
- **Low in fat, sodium, and cholesterol** — essentially zero on all three.

|---------------|----------|

| Carbohydrates | 12 g     |
| — Sugar       | 9 g      |

**Per 1 cup (155 g):** ~77 kcal, 19 g carbs, 13 g sugar, 2.5 g fiber, 1.5 g

| Nutrient  | Amount                              |
|-----------|-------------------------------------|
| Calories  | ~120–140 kcal                       |
| Carbs     | ~29–34 g                            |
| Sugar     | ~28–33 g                            |
| Fiber     | ~0.5 g (most is removed in juicing) |
| Potassium | ~330 mg                             |
| Iron      | ~1 mg                               |

The trade-off with juice: you get more anthocyanins and melatonin per serving,
but lose nearly all the fiber and get a more concentrated sugar load.

## Core Benefits

### Anti-inflammatory & antioxidant

The high anthocyanin concentration (highest of any common cherry) inhibits 
COX-1/COX-2 enzymes, reducing inflammatory cascades. Some studies compare the 
effect to low-dose ibuprofen. This is the single most well-supported benefit.

### Muscle recovery & exercise performance  

A 2021 meta-analysis (14 studies, ~294 people) found moderate benefits for 
strength/power recovery. Marathon runners who drank tart cherry juice for 5 days 
before a race recovered strength faster and showed ~12% less muscle pain. A 2025 
review found ~9% greater strength recovery vs. placebo.

### Sleep  

Tart cherries (*Prunus cerasus*, especially the **Montmorency** variety) support
sleep through a combination of compounds working together:
  - **Melatonin** — They're one of the few natural food sources of this
    sleep-regulating hormone (about 2–13 ng per gram). The amount is small, but
    studies show consuming tart cherry juice significantly raises urinary
    melatonin levels.
  - **Tryptophan** — An essential amino acid the body converts to serotonin and
    then to melatonin, giving your body raw materials to produce *more* of its own
    melatonin.
  - **Procyanidin B-2** — A compound that inhibits the IDO enzyme, which normally
    breaks down tryptophan, leaving more available for melatonin production.
  - **Anthocyanins** — Anti-inflammatory antioxidants that reduce inflammation, a
    known disruptor of sleep architecture.

- **Gout & arthritis** — Associated with fewer gout flares (lower uric acid).
  The evidence here is observational; no large controlled trial has confirmed it
  yet, but the anti-inflammatory mechanism is plausible.

- **Cardiometabolic health** — Modest effects on lowering blood pressure,
  improving lipid profiles, and modulating blood glucose. The polyphenols
  support endothelial function.

- **Gut health** — Contains fermentable fibers and small amounts of
  fructooligosaccharides (FOS) that may act as mild prebiotics, supporting
  beneficial gut microbial activity.

### Bone health (emerging)  

Novel research suggests a gut–bone axis whereby tart cherry polyphenols may 
enhance bone formation, though this is still early-stage.

### Cognitive function  

Some evidence for improved memory and brain function, but human trials are 
limited.

## Key Caveats

- Most studies are **small and short** (1–2 weeks), though results are
  consistent across them.
- The anti-inflammatory and recovery benefits are the **best-supported**;
  cognitive, bone, and weight-loss claims are preliminary.
- Tart cherries are a **food, not a treatment** — they complement but don't
  replace medical care for gout, cardiovascular disease, or chronic pain.
