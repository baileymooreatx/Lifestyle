# Immunoglobulin A (IgA)

**Immunoglobulin** is the protein class that makes up all antibodies, IgA, IgG,
IgM, IgD, and IgE. The letter suffix (A, G, M, D, E) designates the specific
**isotype**, determined by the constant region of the heavy chain. "Ig" is the
abbreviation for Immunoglobulin. "A" was assigned historically. IgA was the
first isotype isolated and characterized, in the 1960s by Kimball and Mitchell.

**IgA** is the most abundantly produced antibody isotype in the body — **3–5 g
are secreted into the intestinal lumen alone each day**, exceeding all other
isotypes combined. It exists in two distinct molecular forms depending on
location.

| Form                               | Location                                                   | Structure                                                   | Role                                         |
|------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------------|-------------------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------|
| **Monomeric IgA**                  | Serum, blood                                               | Single IgA monomer (2 heavy + 2 light chains)               | Circulating; opsonization; less well-defined |
| **Dimeric / Secretory IgA (sIgA)** | Mucosal secretions (gut, respiratory, saliva, tears, milk) | 2 IgA monomers + **J chain** + **secretory component (SC)** | Mucosal immunity; immune exclusion           |

## Secretory IgA (sIgA)

Mucosal epithelial cells have two distinct surfaces. They are polarized.

* The **basolateral** surface is the side that faces away from the lumen and
  toward the body's internal environment.
* The **apical** surface is the side that faces the [lumen](GutLumen.md), the
  open space or cavity on the "outside" of the tissue.

A **polymeric immunoglobulin receptor (pIgR)** is the transmembrane protein on 
the basolateral surface of mucosal epithelial cells. The pIGR binds dimeric IgA 
(or pentameric IgM) via its J chain, mediates transcytosis, transfer across the 
cell, and — after protein splitting at the apical surface — donates its 
extracellular domain (the **secretory component**) to form sIgA.

The dimeric IgA produced by a plasma cell in the lamina propria binds pIgR
on this basolateral side, is internalized, shuttled across the cell
(transcytosis), and released at the apical surface into the gut lumen as sIgA.

### Structure of sIgA  

sIgA is a four-part complex.

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│  IgA monomer 1 ─── J chain ─── IgA monomer 2    │  ← dimeric IgA (dIgA)
│         │                              │        │
│         └──────── SC (secretory component) ─────┘  ← cleaved pIgR ectodomain
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

- **J chain** (~27 kDa): A polypeptide produced by plasma cells that covalently
  links the two IgA Fc tailpieces. Its presence is the signal that marks the
  molecule for polymeric immunoglobulin receptor (pIgR) recognition.
- **Secretory component (SC)** (~70 kDa): The cleaved extracellular domain of
  the **polymeric immunoglobulin receptor (pIgR)**. It remains covalently
  attached to dIgA after transcytosis, providing:
    - **Proteolytic protection** — shields the antibody from digestive enzymes
      in the lumen
    - **Mucus anchoring** — SC's heavy glycosylation (up to 20% of its mass)
      allows non-covalent binding to the mucus layer
    - **Innate recognition** — free SC (without IgA) can bind and exclude
      bacteria on its own

### The Transcytosis Pathway (pIgR-Mediated)

This is the unique "one-way street" that delivers IgA from the tissue into the
lumen.

```
1. Plasma cell in lamina propria produces dIgA (with J chain)
         ↓
2. dIgA binds pIgR on the BASOLATERAL surface of epithelial cells
         ↓
3. Endocytosis → transcytosis across the cell
         ↓
4. At the APICAL (luminal) surface, pIgR is proteolytically cleaved
         ↓
5. dIgA + SC released into the lumen = sIgA
   (pIgR transmembrane + cytoplasmic tails are degraded)
```

**Key points:**

- Only **polymeric, J chain-containing** IgA (and IgM) is recognized by pIgR.
  Monomeric IgA cannot be transported this way.
- pIgR is expressed on the basolateral surface of mucosal epithelial cells
  throughout the GI, respiratory, and urogenital tracts, and in exocrine glands
- Mice lacking pIgR have no sIgA in secretions, show altered microbiota, and are
  susceptible to mucosal infections

### Functions of sIgA

**1. Immune Exclusion** (the primary function)

- sIgA cross-links pathogens, toxins, and food antigens in the mucus layer
- Prevents their adhesion to and penetration of the epithelium
- Trapped material is cleared by **peristalsis** (gut) or **mucociliary
  escalation** (respiratory tract)
- This is a **non-inflammatory** mechanism — unlike IgG/IgM, sIgA does NOT
  activate complement or recruit neutrophils

**2. Microbiome Homeostasis**

- sIgA coats commensal bacteria (a process called **bacterial opsonization**)
  without killing them
- This "decorates" the microbiota, preventing overgrowth of any single species
  and limiting translocation
- The sIgA repertoire is highly **individual-specific** — each person's sIgA
  profile reflects their unique microbiome
- sIgA–antigen complexes are retro-transcytosed back across the epithelium (via
  pIgR or FcαRI) to **dendritic cells** in the lamina propria, where they
  promote **tolerogenic** (Treg-inducing) signaling rather than inflammation

**3. Anti-Inflammatory Signaling**

- sIgA–antigen complexes taken up by mucosal DCs **downregulate** local T cell
  activation
- Reduces expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines in the gut
- Promotes expression of tight-junction proteins and TSLP (thymic stromal
  lymphopoietin)

**4. Intracellular Neutralization**

- Some viruses (e.g., **rotavirus**) are internalized by enterocytes; sIgA can
  neutralize them *inside* the cell before they replicate
- This is distinct from immune exclusion (which blocks entry)

**5. Protection of the Neonate**

- **Breast milk** is rich in sIgA — the primary source of mucosal antibody for
  infants whose own IgA production is immature
- Protects against NEC (necrotizing enterocolitis) and enteric infections in the
  first months of life

## IgA Subclasses (Human)

| Subclass | Feature                                                                                                                                                                           |
|----------|-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **IgA1** | Has an extended hinge region with a **cleavage site** between CH2 and CH3; susceptible to bacterial proteases (e.g., *H. pylori*, group A strep) — cleavage is a virulence factor |
| **IgA2** | Shorter hinge; **resistant to proteolysis**; predominant in the gut                                                                                                               |

Both bind FcαRI (the IgA receptor on neutrophils, monocytes, and epithelial
cells) and pIgR.

## Clinical Relevance

| Condition                                                                   | IgA/sIgA Pathology                                                                                                                   |
|-----------------------------------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Selective IgA deficiency** (most common primary immunodeficiency, ~1:600) | Absent/very low serum IgA; often asymptomatic but ↑ risk of mucosal infections, autoimmune disease, anaphylaxis on blood transfusion |
| **Common Variable Immunodeficiency**                                        | Low all isotypes including IgA → recurrent sinopulmonary infections                                                                  |
| **Celiac disease**                                                          | Anti-tTG and anti-endomysial IgA; total IgA may be low (confounding serology)                                                        |
| **IgA nephropathy (Berger's disease)**                                      | Galactose-deficient IgA1 → immune complexes deposit in renal mesangium → hematuria                                                   |
| **IBD**                                                                     | Altered sIgA repertoire; loss of commensal coating → dysbiosis amplification                                                         |
| **Mucosal vaccines**                                                        | Designed to induce sIgA (oral/nasal delivery) for local protection — e.g., rotavirus vaccine, typhoid                                |

## How IgA Is Connected

- **Microbiome:** sIgA is the molecular interface between the immune system and
  the commensal microbiota. It "decorates" bacteria, shapes community 
  composition, and feeds tolerogenic signals to DCs. Loss of sIgA (pIgR
  knockout, IgA deficiency) → dysbiosis → inflammation
- **Tolerance paradox:** sIgA is how the immune system *tolerates* 10¹⁴
  commensals without mounting the same inflammatory response it would mount
  against a pathogen. It neutralizes and excludes without activating complement
  or recruiting inflammatory cells.
- **B cells:** sIgA production requires the full T-dependent GC reaction (Tfh →
  CD40L + TGF-β/IL-2 → class-switch to IgA → dimerization with J chain → pIgR
  transcytosis). This is why infants with immature GCs have low sIgA.
- Allergies and arthritis both involve a failure of mucosal/systemic 
  tolerance, the same regulatory logic that sIgA embodies at the gut barrier, 
  but expressed (or broken) in other tissues.
