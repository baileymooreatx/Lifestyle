# Ligand

Receptors bind to specific ligands. A ligand is **the thing that binds** — 
whether it's a neurotransmitter activating a brain receptor, a pathogen's LPS 
triggering TLR4, or an antibody recognizing an antigen. The word simply means 
"the binding partner" in any molecular interaction.

## Key properties

| **Property**               | **Meaning**                                                                                                           |
|----------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Affinity**               | How tightly the ligand binds (measured as Kd — the concentration at which 50% of receptors are occupied)              |
| **Specificity**            | How selectively it binds one target vs. others                                                                        |
| **Agonist vs. antagonist** | Agonist: binds and *activates* the receptor. Antagonist: binds but *blocks* activation (or blocks the natural ligand) |
| **Reversibility**          | In biology, ligand–receptor binding is almost always reversible (unlike covalent enzyme–substrate reactions)          |

## Two contexts where "Ligand" means slightly different things

### Biochemistry / Pharmacology  

The *smaller* molecule that binds to a larger protein 
(e.g., dopamine → dopamine receptor; insulin → insulin receptor; 
a drug → its target). 
The ligand is the "key," the receptor is the "lock."  

### Inorganic / Coordination Chemistry  

An atom, ion, or molecule (often with a lone pair) that donates electrons to a 
central metal atom (e.g., Cl⁻, NH₃, H₂O binding to Fe²⁺ in hemoglobin). This is 
the original meaning; the biochemistry usage is a broader extension.
