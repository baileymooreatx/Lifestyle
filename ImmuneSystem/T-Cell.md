## T Cells

**T cells** (T lymphocytes) are the central orchestrators of **adaptive 
immunity**. They originate in the **bone marrow** (like B cells) but mature in 
the **thymus**, hence the "T." They cannot recognize free-floating antigens; 
they require peptides to be **presented on MHC molecules** by another cell, 
making them inherently "cell-to-cell" communicators.

### Development in the Thymus

Thymic progenitors, from bone marrow, undergo a stepwise maturation.

1. **Double-negative** (CD4⁻CD8⁻) → rearrange TCR β chain (RAG1/2)
2. **Double-positive** (CD4⁺CD8⁺) → rearrange TCR α chain; undergo **positive
   selection** (must bind self-MHC weakly) and **negative selection** (must NOT
   bind self-peptide strongly)
3. **Single-positive** → CD4⁺ (recognize MHC II) or CD8⁺ (recognize MHC I)
4. Exit to peripheral lymphoid organs as **naïve T cells**

This selection process is the immune system's "fingerprint" system. It is what 
prevents most T cells from attacking the body's own tissues. When it fails → 
**autoimmunity** (rheumatoid arthritis, lupus, etc.).

### The T Cell Receptor (TCR)

| Component                           | Role                                                                                      |
|-------------------------------------|-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **TCR αβ chains** (most T cells)    | Recognize a specific peptide (8–17 aa) bound to MHC                                       |
| **CD3 complex** (γ, δ, ε, ζ chains) | Signal transduction; contains **ITAMs** (Immunoreceptor Tyrosine-based Activation Motifs) |
| **CD4** (helper)                    | Binds MHC II; recruits Lck kinase                                                         |
| **CD8** (cytotoxic)                 | Binds MHC I; stabilizes TCR–pMHC interaction                                              |

The TCR is generated by **V(D)J recombination** (Chapter 12 in the book) — the
same combinatorial mechanism that gives B cells their near-infinite antibody
diversity. A healthy adult carries ~10⁷–10⁸ unique TCR clones.

### Three Signals Required for Activation

| Signal                        | Source                          | Molecules                                   | Function                                                                           |
|-------------------------------|---------------------------------|---------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Signal 1** (Antigen)        | TCR → pMHC on APC               | TCR + CD3 ITAMs → Lck → ZAP-70 → LAT/SLP-76 | Antigen-specific recognition; initiates transcription (IL-2, etc.)                 |
| **Signal 2** (Co-stimulation) | CD28 (T cell) → CD80/CD86 (APC) | PI3K–AKT, MAPK/ERK                          | Prevents **anergy** (functional inactivation); promotes survival and proliferation |
| **Signal 3** (Cytokines)      | APC-derived cytokines           | IL-12, IL-4, IL-6, TGF-β, IL-23             | Directs **lineage commitment** (Th1, Th2, Th17, Treg, etc.)                        |

Without Signal 2, a T cell that sees its antigen becomes **anergic** (
unresponsive) — a key tolerance mechanism. This is the molecular basis of the "
brakes" exploited by cancer (PD-1/PD-L1, Chapter 40 in the book) and targeted by
**checkpoint inhibitors** (nivolumab, pembrolizumab).

### Major Subsets & Functions

| Subset                      | Co-receptor                   | MHC                         | Key Cytokines     | Primary Function                                                                       |
|-----------------------------|-------------------------------|-----------------------------|-------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **CD8⁺ Cytotoxic T (CTL)**  | CD8                           | I                           | IFN-γ, TNF-α      | Kill infected/tumor cells via **perforin/granzyme** and **Fas-FasL**                   |
| **Th1**                     | CD4                           | II                          | IFN-γ, IL-2       | Activate macrophages; anti-intracellular pathogens                                     |
| **Th2**                     | CD4                           | II                          | IL-4, IL-5, IL-13 | Activate eosinophils/mast cells; **B cell class-switching** to IgE/IgA; anti-helminths |
| **Th17**                    | CD4                           | II                          | IL-17, IL-22      | Recruit neutrophils; anti-extracellular bacteria/fungi                                 |
| **Tfh** (follicular helper) | CD4                           | II                          | IL-21, IL-4       | B cell germinal center reactions → high-affinity antibodies                            |
| **Treg**                    | CD4 (FOXP3⁺)                  | II                          | IL-10, TGF-β      | **Suppress** immune responses; maintain self-tolerance                                 |
| **γδ T cells**              | Neither CD4 nor CD8 (usually) | MHC-independent (CD1, etc.) | IFN-γ, TNF-α      | Rapid innate-like responses; tissue-resident (skin, gut)                               |
| **NKT cells**               | CD4 (usually)                 | CD1d                        | IFN-γ, IL-4       | Bridge innate/adaptive; lipid antigen recognition                                      |

**Memory T cells** (formed after resolution of infection):

- **Central memory (Tcm):** reside in lymphoid organs; proliferate on
  re-challenge
- **Effector memory (Tem):** patrol blood and peripheral tissues; rapid effector
  function
- **Tissue-resident memory (Trm):** permanently stationed in the tissue where
  they first encountered the pathogen (skin, gut, lung)

### How They Connect to Prior Topics

| Connection                        | Mechanism                                                                                                                                                                             |
|-----------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **TLRs/NLRs → T cells**           | TLR/NLR activation on dendritic cells upregulates CD80/86 (Signal 2) and produces cytokines (Signal 3) that polarize naïve T cells                                                    |
| **Microbiome → T cells**          | Commensal SCFAs (butyrate) promote **Treg** differentiation; NOD2 signaling in Paneth cells maintains barrier integrity; dysbiosis → loss of Treg tone → autoimmunity                 |
| **Autoimmunity (Bob, Merredith)** | Failure of negative selection + loss of Treg suppression → autoreactive T cells escape and attack self-tissue (joints, skin, etc.)                                                    |
| **Cancer immunotherapy (Jason)**  | Tumors express **PD-L1** → binds PD-1 on CD8⁺ T cells → T cell inactivation ("exhaustion"). Checkpoint inhibitors (nivolumab) block this interaction, reactivating CTLs against tumor |
| **Allergy (Linda)**               | Th2 skewing → IL-4/IL-13 → IgE class-switching → mast cell degranulation on re-exposure → anaphylaxis                                                                                 |

### T Cell Exhaustion & Senescence

Under **chronic antigen stimulation** (persistent viral infection like HIV/HBV,
or tumor microenvironment), CD8⁺ T cells progressively:

- Upregulate **inhibitory receptors**: PD-1, LAG-3, TIM-3, TIGIT
- Lose effector function (↓ perforin, ↓ IFN-γ)
- Undergo a transcriptional shift driven by **TOX** (master exhaustion
  transcription factor)
- Progress from **progenitor-exhausted (Tpx)** → **terminally exhausted (Tex)**

This is precisely the state that **checkpoint inhibitors** reverse in cancer
patients — by blocking PD-1/PD-L1, they "unbrake" exhausted T cells, allowing
them to resume killing. In chronic infections, however, full reactivation is
harder, and exhaustion is more entrenched.

### Clinical Relevance at a Glance

| Condition                 | T Cell Pathology                                                 |
|---------------------------|------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **HIV/AIDS**              | Progressive loss of CD4⁺ T cells → collapse of adaptive immunity |
| **Rheumatoid arthritis**  | Th1/Th17 autoreactive T cells in synovium                        |
| **Type 1 Diabetes**       | CD8⁺ T cells destroy pancreatic β cells                          |
| **Multiple Sclerosis**    | Th1/Th17 cells attack myelin                                     |
| **SLE**                   | TLR7/9-driven B cell help + loss of Treg control                 |
| **Cancer**                | T cell exhaustion; PD-1/PD-L1 axis exploited by tumor            |
| **Graft rejection**       | Recipient T cells recognize donor MHC as "non-self"              |
| **SCID (thymic aplasia)** | No thymic selection → no functional T cells                      |

### Key Takeaway

T cells are the **decision-making layer** of adaptive immunity. They integrate
information from innate sensors (TLRs, NLRs), the microbiome, and the tissue
microenvironment to determine *whether* to respond, *how strongly*, and *which
effector program* to deploy. The entire "central dilemma" of the immune system —
protect without destroying self — is ultimately resolved (or broken) at the
level of T cell selection, activation, and regulation.
