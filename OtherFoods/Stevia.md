# Stevia

High-purity stevia is considered safe for most people. The FDA has granted
it "Generally Recognized as Safe" (GRAS) status, and global health authorities (
EFSA, JECFA, WHO) have reviewed decades of studies finding no evidence of
toxicity, cancer risk, or organ damage at normal intake levels (acceptable daily
intake: 4 mg/kg body weight).  

In short, for most people, stevia is a **safer alternative to sugar** with
negligible calories and no impact on blood glucose. The main risk comes not from
stevia itself but from the other ingredients mixed into commercial products.  

## Caveats

- **Digestive side effects:** Some people report bloating, gas, or nausea,
  especially if the product contains sugar alcohols like erythritol or xylitol.
- **Additives matter:** Many commercial stevia products are blended with
  fillers, dextrose, or erythritol. Erythritol specifically has been linked to a
  higher risk of heart attack and stroke in recent studies. Read the label. 
  Pure steviol glycosides (Reb-A) are the safest form.
- **Whole-leaf stevia** is *not* FDA-approved due to insufficient safety data;
  only the highly purified extract is approved.
- **Blood pressure & interactions:** Stevia can lower blood pressure and may
  interact with blood pressure or blood sugar medications.

## Gut Microbiome

At normal consumption levels, human studies show stevia is largely gut-neutral.
The most relevant evidence comes from two human clinical trials.

- A **12-week randomized trial** (published in *Nutrients*, 2024) found **no
  significant changes** in overall microbiome composition, alpha or beta
  diversity, after daily stevia consumption at real-life doses. One nuance:
  stevia did affect microbiome *function* — specifically fatty acid
  biosynthesis — without changing which bacteria were present.
- A **4-week crossover study** (published in *The American Journal of Clinical
  Nutrition*, 2024) compared a steviol glycoside beverage at 25% of the ADI to
  sucrose and found **no significant differences** in gut microflora, SCFA
  production, or any cardiometabolic markers.

**Where the concerns come from:**

- **In vitro (lab) studies** from Ben-Gurion University showed steviol
  glycosides can **disrupt quorum sensing**, the chemical communication 
  between gut bacteria, without killing them. This is a subtle effect and 
  hasn't been confirmed in humans.
- **Animal studies** are mixed. A mouse model on a high-fat diet showed stevia
  altered microbiota in ways similar to saccharin, but this was in the context
  of an already dysbiotic gut.
- A **2026 Cambridge study** found that isosteviol (a stevia metabolite)
  *combined with the antidepressant duloxetine* impaired certain gut bacteria —
  but this is a drug interaction, not stevia alone.

**Possible positive effects:** Some in vitro work suggests stevia may have mild
**prebiotic properties**, supporting growth of *Lactobacillus* and
*Bifidobacterium*, though this hasn't been strongly confirmed in humans.

The human evidence is reassuring. Stevia doesn't appear to meaningfully shift 
the gut microbiome at typical doses, and it may be more "gut-neutral" than older 
artificial sweeteners like saccharin or sucralose, which have shown more 
pronounced effects on both microbiota and glucose tolerance.
