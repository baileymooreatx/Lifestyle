# Salmon

Salmon is a nutritional powerhouse, rich in omega-3 fatty acids (EPA and DHA), 
high-quality complete protein, vitamin D, B vitamins (B12, B6, niacin), 
selenium, potassium, and [astaxanthin](../Supplements/Astaxanthin.md) (the 
powerful antioxidant that gives salmon its pink color).

Key health benefits include:

- **Heart health** – Omega-3s lower blood pressure, reduce triglycerides, and
  decrease "bad" LDL cholesterol.
- **Brain & cognitive function** – DHA supports memory, learning, and may reduce
  risk of cognitive decline.
- **Bone strength** – One serving provides more than half the daily recommended
  vitamin D, which helps calcium absorption.
- **Muscle growth & satiety** – ~20–25 g of complete protein per 100 g serving
  supports muscle mass and helps regulate appetite.
- **Anti-inflammatory** – Omega-3s and astaxanthin work together to reduce
  systemic inflammation.
- **Vision & skin health** – Astaxanthin and vitamin A protect eyes from
  oxidative damage and support skin elasticity.
- **Immune & thyroid support** – Selenium and B12 contribute to immune function
  and thyroid health.

Eating **two servings of oily fish like salmon per week** is widely recommended
by health authorities (NHS, Cleveland Clinic, Healthline) to capture these
benefits.

### Notes  

- Values are for **raw** fish; cooking (especially dry-heat) reduces water
  content and concentrates nutrients slightly per gram, while also reducing
  total serving weight.
- Sockeye is the **leanest** of the Pacific salmon species — its fat content (~
  8.6 g/100 g) is notably lower than king (~15 g) or farmed Atlantic (~12 g),
  but it still delivers robust omega-3 levels.
- The **omega-3:omega-6 ratio** is approximately **10:1**, far superior to the
  typical Western diet ratio of 15–20:1.

## Omega-3 Fatty Acids (EPA & DHA)

Salmon is one of the most efficient dietary sources of the long-chain omega-3s *
*EPA** (eicosapentaenoic acid) and **DHA** (docosahexaenoic acid). A 3.5 oz (100
g) serving delivers roughly **1.5–2.5 g** of combined EPA+DHA, depending on the
species. Wild king (Chinook) salmon leads at **1,800–2,200 mg** per 6 oz
serving, while wild sockeye provides **1,200–1,600 mg**.

Unlike plant-based omega-3s (ALA), EPA and DHA are **bioactive** — they don't
require metabolic conversion, so the body uses them directly.

### Cardiovascular Mechanisms  

- EPA and DHA **reduce triglyceride levels** by slowing the liver's production
  (studies show reductions of up to 25–30%)
- They **modestly lower blood pressure** and improve endothelial (blood vessel)
  function
- They **decrease platelet aggregation**, reducing clot risk
- They **stabilize cardiac cell membranes** and may help prevent arrhythmias
- A **2023 meta-analysis of 18 studies** confirmed that regular salmon
  consumption is associated with lower cardiovascular disease risk
- The body converts omega-3s into **nitric oxide**, which relaxes blood vessels
  and improves blood flow and oxygen delivery

### Anti-inflammatory Action  

EPA and DHA produce **specialized pro-resolving mediators** (resolvins,
protectins) that actively resolve inflammation rather than just suppressing it.
This is distinct from anti-inflammatory drugs. Clinical trials show benefits for
**rheumatoid arthritis joint stiffness** at doses of 2.7+ g/day. A 2025 review
also suggests omega-3s may **reduce inflammation in fat tissue**, which
otherwise contributes to obesity.

### Brain & Cognition  

DHA is a **structural component of neuronal membranes** and makes up ~60% of the
infant prefrontal cortex. A study in the journal *Neurology* found that people
who eat fatty fish regularly have **more gray matter** in their brains,
associated with better cognitive function. Adequate DHA intake is linked to
reduced risk of **age-related cognitive decline, Alzheimer's, and Parkinson's
disease**. The NHS specifically advises pregnant women to consume oily fish
within safe limits to support **fetal brain development**.

### Mental health  

Cleveland Clinic notes that even **one weekly serving** of salmon is associated
with a **lower risk of depression**, especially in women. This is partly
attributed to vitamin D, which is critical for mood regulation and sleep.

## High-Quality Complete Protein

A 100 g cooked serving delivers **~22–25 g of complete protein** (wild salmon
~25.4 g, farmed ~22.1 g), providing **all nine essential amino acids** necessary
for muscle repair, enzyme formation, and immune support.

- The protein is **low in saturated fat** — wild salmon has just **~1 g** per
  serving, compared to 5–6 g in beef
- Protein from salmon promotes **satiety** (feeling full), which supports weight
  management
- A 2025 review found that omega-3s from salmon may also **improve lipid levels
  and reduce inflammation in fat tissue**, compounding the weight-management
  benefit
- For comparison, a 6 oz serving of wild salmon provides **34–40 g** of protein

## Vitamin D

Salmon is one of the **few natural food sources** of vitamin D, a nutrient most
people don't get enough of.

| Source                     | Vitamin D per serving | % Daily Value |
|:---------------------------|----------------------:|--------------:|
| Wild sockeye (100 g)       |     16.75 µg (670 IU) |          ~84% |
| Farmed Atlantic (100 g)    |     13.15 µg (526 IU) |          ~66% |
| Wild salmon (178 g / 6 oz) |               23.3 µg |         ~117% |

Wild-caught salmon contains up to **25% more vitamin D** than farmed salmon.
Vitamin D is essential for:

- **Calcium absorption and bone formation** — one serving covers 70–80% of daily
  needs
- **Immune function**
- **Mood regulation and sleep** — research shows vitamin D is critical for
  mental health
- A 2016 randomized trial (*Oncotarget*) found that dietary vitamin D from
  salmon **reduced bone resorption**

## B Vitamins (B12, B6, Niacin/B3, B5)

Salmon is an **excellent source of vitamin B12**, which is essential for:

- **Nerve function** and red blood cell production
- **DNA synthesis**
- Preventing **megaloblastic anemia**

A 2022 randomized controlled trial confirmed that salmon-based protein 
**reliably increases serum B12 and selenium concentrations**. Niacin (B3)
supports energy metabolism and cardiovascular health, while B6 plays roles in
neurotransmitter synthesis.

## Selenium

A 3.5 oz serving provides **35–47 µg** of selenium, which is **75–85% of the
daily value**.

Selenium is critical for:

- **Thyroid hormone regulation** — maintaining physiological selenium levels is
  a prerequisite for preventing thyroid disease
- **Antioxidant defense** — it's a cofactor for glutathione peroxidase, a major
  cellular antioxidant
- **Immune function** — a 2023 *Journal of Trace Elements in Medicine and
  Biology* study found selenium-rich foods enhance **T-cell and antibody
  response**, reducing infection risk
- **Bone health** — supports the creation of new bone tissue

## Astaxanthin (The "King of Antioxidants")

Astaxanthin is the **carotenoid pigment** that gives salmon its distinctive
pink-orange color. It's a powerful antioxidant that is **significantly more
potent** than vitamin C, vitamin E, and coenzyme Q10 at neutralizing free
radicals.

**Key research findings:**

- A 2022 study (Lu et al., *PubMed*) found that **two servings of salmon per
  week over five weeks** significantly elevated plasma astaxanthin levels in
  humans
- Wild-caught salmon contains **2 to 4 times more astaxanthin** than farmed 
  varieties (wild sockeye: ~3–4 mg per 100 g)
- **Cooking does not destroy it**. A 2022 study confirmed baking had no
  significant effect on astaxanthin concentrations
- **Heart health:** Animal research suggests it helps prevent **fatty plaque and
  cholesterol buildup** in arteries
- **Blood pressure:** Doses of 12 mg significantly lowered **diastolic blood
  pressure**, with stronger effects in patients vs. healthy individuals
- **Inflammation:** A meta-analysis of 12 trials (380 participants) found
  astaxanthin significantly reduced inflammatory markers (IL-6, malondialdehyde)
  in type 2 diabetes patients
- **Skin:** A 2023 *Nutrients* clinical trial found people consuming
  salmon-derived astaxanthin had **significant improvements in skin hydration
  and wrinkle depth** within 12 weeks
- **Eye health:** May help prevent **age-related macular degeneration**,
  cataracts, and eye fatigue
- **Neuroprotection:** Works in conjunction with omega-3s to **slow brain
  aging**

## Potassium & Other Minerals

Salmon is a good source of **potassium**, which helps:

- **Regulate blood pressure** by counteracting sodium's effects
- **Prevent fluid retention**
- Support proper **muscle and nerve function**

Combined with the omega-3s, potassium in salmon provides a **dual mechanism**
for cardiovascular protection.

## Wild vs. Farmed  

| Nutrient            | Wild Salmon                   | Farmed Salmon          |
|:--------------------|:------------------------------|:-----------------------|
| Protein (per 100 g) | ~25.4 g                       | ~22.1 g                |
| Vitamin D           | Up to 25% higher              | Lower (varies by feed) |
| Astaxanthin         | 2 to 4 times more             | Lower                  |
| EPA + DHA           | Higher (especially wild king) | Lower                  |
| Saturated fat       | ~1 g per serving              | Slightly higher        |
| Calories            | ~150–170 kcal                 | ~180–200 kcal          |

A 2022 study confirmed that **wild-caught salmon has significantly higher**
astaxanthin, EPA, and DHA concentrations than farmed salmon, while farmed has
higher ALA and arachidonic acid.

## Practical Tips for Maximizing Benefits

- **Serving size:** The FDA and American Heart Association recommend **two 3.5
  oz (100 g) servings per week** of fatty fish
- **Cooking methods:** Baking, grilling, steaming, and pan-searing retain 
  **70–85% of EPA and DHA** — standard cooking doesn't meaningfully degrade the
  nutrients
- **Frozen vs. fresh:** **Flash-freezing at sea** locks in omega-3s with no
  significant nutritional difference from fresh fish; frozen may actually
  preserve nutrients better than fish in extended transport
- **Omega-3 Index:** This measures EPA+DHA as a percentage of red blood cell
  membrane fatty acids. **8–12% is optimal**; below 4% is associated with higher
  cardiovascular risk
- **Safety:** Up to **5 g/day** of omega-3 is considered safe for adults (EFSA),
  but high doses can increase bleeding time and interact with blood thinners.
  Two to three fish servings per week is well within safe ranges
- **Pregnancy:** The WHO recommends at least **200 mg DHA daily** for pregnant
  women. Wild salmon is on the FDA's **Best Choices list** for pregnancy. A
  Cochrane review of 70 trials found omega-3s associated with **reduced preterm
  birth risk**
- **Children:** DHA supports brain development throughout childhood; EFSA
  recommends **150–250 mg EPA+DHA per day** for children aged 2–12
