# Akkermansia muciniphila

_Akkermansia muciniphila_ is a **Gram-negative, strictly anaerobic, non-motile,
non-spore-forming** bacterium that colonizes the mucus layer lining the human
[intestinal tract](../Body/GutLumen.md). It was first isolated from human feces 
in **2004** by Muriel Derrien and Willem de Vos at Wageningen University in the 
Netherlands.

**Key facts:**

- **Name origin:** *muciniphila* = Latin *mucin* (mucus) + Greek *phila*
  (loving) — it literally feeds on mucus. The genus *Akkermansia* is named after
  Dutch microbiologist Antoon Akkermans.
- **Prevalence:** Found in >90% of healthy adults, typically comprising **1–5%**
  of the total fecal microbiota (~8 × 10⁹ cells per gram of feces). Levels
  decline with age and are reduced in obesity, type 2 diabetes, and chronic
  inflammatory conditions.
- **Diet:** Uses mucin as its sole source of carbon, nitrogen, and energy. It
  degrades the mucus layer but simultaneously stimulates goblet cells to produce
  *more* mucin, creating a net positive effect on barrier thickness.
- **Genomic diversity:** Recent analyses (2025) have identified at least **31
  species** within the *Akkermansia* genus, with *A. muciniphila* itself split
  into at least two phylogroups (AmIa, AmIb) with distinct metabolic profiles.
- **Commercial status:** In June 2025, Danone acquired The Akkermansia Company
  (TAC), the Belgian firm that developed the MucT™ strain for use as a
  next-generation probiotic.

In short, it's a keystone symbiont that lives at the interface between the gut
lining and the lumen, and its abundance is one of the most consistently studied
microbial biomarkers for metabolic health.

## Beneficial Affects of Akkermansia  

### Metabolic Health (strongest human evidence)

- **Insulin sensitivity** — the 2019 *Nature Medicine* randomized controlled 
  trial (RCT) (Depommier et al.) showed ~30% improvement in insulin sensitivity 
  with 3 months of pasteurized *A. muciniphila*. A 2025 RCT in overweight/obese 
  T2D patients confirmed improved glycemic control and insulin sensitivity, with
  benefits concentrated in those with **low baseline levels**.
- **Weight & fat mass** — animal studies consistently show reduced adiposity and
  body weight on high-fat diets. Human data shows trends toward reduced weight,
  waist circumference, and fat mass, though most trials haven't reached
  statistical significance for weight alone.
- **Lipid profile** — reduced total cholesterol and triglycerides in human
  trials.
- **[GLP-1 & satiety signaling](../Body/SatietyHormones.md)** — *A. muciniphila*
  (particularly via its outer membrane protein Amuc_1100) stimulates GLP-1 and 
  PYY secretion while reducing leptin, enhancing satiety and reducing appetite.

### Gut Barrier & Digestive Health

- **Mucus layer reinforcement** — by feeding on mucin, it stimulates goblet
  cells to produce *more* mucin, thickening the protective layer.
- **Reduced intestinal permeability** — strengthens tight junctions (ZO-1,
  occludin), reducing "leaky gut" and endotoxemia (LPS leaking into the
  bloodstream).
- **SCFA production** — generates short-chain fatty acids (butyrate, propionate,
  isobutyrate) that nourish colonocytes and maintain barrier integrity.

### Anti-inflammatory & Immune Effects

- **Reduces pro-inflammatory cytokines** (TNF-α, IL-6, CRP) and
  endotoxemia-driven systemic inflammation.
- **Modulates immune tone** — promotes regulatory T cell differentiation,
  increases IL-10 and TGF-β, and shifts tryptophan metabolism toward
  anti-inflammatory metabolites (indole-3-lactic acid, 5-HIAA) while suppressing
  the pro-inflammatory kynurenine pathway.
- **Colonization resistance** — helps protect against pathogens like
  *C. difficile* by enriching butyrate producers.

### Cardiovascular & Liver

- **Atherosclerosis** — animal models show reduced atherosclerotic plaque via
  decreased vascular inflammation and calcification (propionate-mediated).
- **NAFLD / fatty liver** — activates the hepatic LKB1-AMPK axis, increasing
  lipid oxidation and reducing hepatic fat accumulation.

### Emerging / Preclinical Areas

- **Cancer immunotherapy** — higher *A. muciniphila* levels are associated with
  better response to immune checkpoint inhibitors (enhanced IL-12 and T-cell
  activation).
- **Neurological** — early data suggests it may preserve blood–brain barrier
  integrity in aging and modulate α-synuclein oligomerization (Parkinson's
  relevance).
- **Bone health** — metabolites from *A. muciniphila* support osteocyte
  differentiation and may protect against postmenopausal bone loss.
- **Aging** — declines in *A. muciniphila* with age are linked to increased
  chronic inflammation and metabolic deterioration; supplementation in aged mice
  improved metabolic markers.

### Important Caveats

- Most mechanistic data comes from **animal models**; human RCTs are still
  limited in number and size.
- Benefits appear to be **baseline-dependent** — people who start with low *A.
  muciniphila* tend to benefit most.
- A larger 2026 metabolic syndrome trial did **not** meet its primary
  insulin-sensitivity endpoint across the whole group, though subgroup benefits
  were seen — reinforcing that this isn't a universal fix.
- The 2025 Chinese RCT (largest to date) found **live** cells were more
  effective than pasteurized for metabolic parameters, partially challenging the
  earlier finding that pasteurized was superior.

## Beneficial Factors  

The core principle: *Akkermansia* feeds on the gut mucus layer, so anything that
thickens or maintains that layer creates a favorable niche for it to thrive.  

The most evidence-supported ways to increase Akkermansia muciniphila are:  

- **Polyphenol-rich foods**  
  The strongest dietary signal. [Cranberry](../Food/Fruits/Cranberry.md) 
  proanthocyanidins (PACs), red grapes, pomegranate (ellagitannins), berries,
  [green tea](../Food/Drinks/Matcha.md),
  [dark chocolate](../Food/OtherFoods/Cacao.md), and
  [extra-virgin olive oil](../Food/Fats/ExtraVirginOliveOil.md) all have data
  showing increased *Akkermansia* abundance, mainly in animal and *in vitro*
  models with some human confirmation (e.g., pomegranate extract in healthy
  volunteers).
- **Prebiotic fiber**  
  [Inulin](./Inulin.md) (chicory root, Jerusalem artichoke, asparagus, 
  [garlic](../Food/Vegetables/Garlic.md), [onions](../Food/Vegetables/Onion.md), 
  leeks), 
  [fructooligosaccharides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fructooligosaccharide)
  (FOS), and [resistant starch](ResistantStarch.md)
  (cooked-and-cooled potatoes/rice, slightly green bananas, oats). These feed
  [SCFA](../Body/ShortChainFattyAcid.md)-producing bacteria that maintain the
  mucus layer *Akkermansia* depends on. Animal studies consistently show FOS
  promotes *Akkermansia* growth.
- **Omega-3 fatty acids** — a human gut-simulator study found fish oil
  supplementation increased *Akkermansia* abundance. Oily fish 2×/week 
  ([salmon](../Food/Protein/Salmon.md), sardines, mackerel) or a supplement.
- **[Intermittent fasting](../Fasting.md) / extended overnight fasts**
  A small study showed *Akkermansia* abundance rose after a period of daily 
  fasting, though evidence is preliminary.
- **Regular exercise** — moderate physical activity is associated with higher
  *Akkermansia* levels and a more diverse microbiome.
- **Supplements:** Viable *A. muciniphila* probiotic supplements (now
  commercially available) have shown consistent benefit in animal studies for 
  restoring abundance after a high-fat diet. HMOs like 2'-fucosyllactose have 
  *in vitro* and early-life model data as a direct food source for the 
  bacterium. [Berberine](./Berberine.md) has been shown to indirectly stimulate 
  *Akkermansia* by increasing mucin secretion.

## Detrimental Factors  

The following reduce the mucus layer or directly disrupt the microbiome in
ways that suppress *Akkermansia*.

- High [sugar](../Avoid/Sugar.md) diet
- [Ultra-processed foods](../Avoid/Ultra-processedFood.md)
- High fat diet (see below)
- Chronic stress
- Excessive [alcohol](../Avoid/Alcohol.md)
- Unnecessary antibiotic courses all

### How a High-Fat Diet Depletes *Akkermansia*

The effect is **dramatic and relatively fast** in animal models.

- **Magnitude:** A 60% fat Western diet in mice causes a **100-fold decrease**
  in *A. muciniphila* abundance (from ~10⁹/g feces down to ~10⁷/g) within 8
  weeks (Everard et al., 2013).  
- **Timeline:** The decline begins within **4 weeks** and is progressive. One
  long-term study (Plovier et al., 2015) tracked it over months and found levels
  fell to roughly **10,000× lower** than baseline with prolonged feeding.
- **Mechanism (proposed):**
  - HFD reduces **mucin production** and **goblet cell density** in the
    colon — removing *Akkermansia*'s food source and habitat.
  - Increased bile acids and altered lipid composition in the lumen create a
    less favorable environment.
  - The shift toward a higher *Firmicutes:Bacteroidota* ratio reflects broader
    dysbiosis that indirectly suppresses *Akkermansia* niche availability.
  - Reduced fiber intake (inherent to most HFDs) starves SCFA producers that
    help maintain mucus integrity.

#### Important nuance

The effect isn't universal across all models:

- **Sex matters** — a 2022 study found HFD *increased* *Akkermansia* in female
  mice (estrogen-dependent), while reducing it in males.
- **Genetic background matters** — some strains (e.g., C3HeB/FeJ females) showed
  increased abundance on HFD.
- **High-fat + high-sugar** diets have shown mixed results compared to fat
  alone.

#### Recovery

Once the HFD is stopped or interventions are added, *Akkermansia* can be
restored:

- **FOS (0.3 g/day)** fully restored levels back to control within 8 weeks in
  HFD mice.
- **Viable *A. muciniphila* supplementation** (4 × 10⁸ CFU/day) restored
  abundance within 6 weeks.
- **Metformin** consistently increased levels in HFD mice (and in human T2D
  patients).
- **Live *A. muciniphila* MucT** (Danone's strain) counteracted HFD-induced
  mucin 3 depletion and restored goblet cell function within 6 weeks (2026
  study).
