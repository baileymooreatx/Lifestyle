# Choline

Choline is an essential nutrient (recognized by the Institute of Medicine
in 1998) that the body cannot produce in sufficient quantities, so it **must be
obtained from the diet**. Its key benefits include:

- **Brain & nervous system:** It is the precursor to **acetylcholine**, a
  neurotransmitter critical for memory, learning, mood regulation, and muscle
  control. Higher intake is associated with better cognitive performance and a
  lower risk of dementia and Alzheimer's disease. It is also crucial for fetal
  brain and spinal cord development during pregnancy.
- **Liver health:** Choline is required to export fat and cholesterol from the
  liver. Deficiency leads to fat accumulation and **nonalcoholic fatty liver
  disease (NAFLD)**.
- **Cell structure:** It is a component of **phosphatidylcholine** and 
  **sphingomyelin**, two major phospholipids that maintain the structural
  integrity of all cell membranes.
- **Heart health:** Choline helps convert the amino acid **homocysteine** to
  methionine; elevated homocysteine is a risk factor for cardiovascular disease
  and stroke. Higher choline intake has been linked to lower stroke risk.
- **Metabolism & DNA synthesis:** It acts as a methyl donor in one-carbon
  metabolism and works with folate and vitamin B12 to support DNA synthesis and
  gene expression.

Most Americans do not meet the recommended adequate intake (~90% fall short).
Rich dietary sources include eggs, organ meats, fish, soybeans, and poultry.

Here's a deeper dive into each of choline's roles, the mechanisms behind them,
and the current evidence.

## Brain & Cognitive Function

Choline is converted into **acetylcholine**, a neurotransmitter that governs
memory, learning, mood, muscle contraction, pain response, and autonomic
functions like heart rate and breathing. Acetylcholine levels naturally decline
with age, which is partly why cognitive performance dips over time.

A 2023 study from Arizona State University (published in *Aging Cell*) found
that choline-deficient mice developed hallmark Alzheimer's pathologies —
increased **amyloid-β plaques** and **tau tangles** — even in non-transgenic
animals. Choline deficiency also disrupted hippocampal protein networks tied to
learning and memory. Conversely, in Alzheimer's mouse models, lifelong choline
supplementation reduced plaque burden and improved spatial memory.

A 2019 study of ~500 men found that every 50 mg/day increase in 
**phosphatidylcholine** (the most common dietary choline form) was associated
with a **10% reduction in dementia risk**.

**Fetal development** is especially sensitive. Choline is critical for neural
tube closure, hippocampal formation, and epigenetic programming (it modulates
gene expression through methylation). Infants whose mothers consumed ~930 mg/day
of choline during pregnancy showed measurably better attention, memory, and
processing speed than those whose mothers met only the 450 mg recommendation.

## Liver Health

The liver uses choline to synthesize **phosphatidylcholine**, which is packaged
into **VLDL (very-low-density lipoprotein)** particles that transport
triglycerides out of the liver to other tissues. Without enough choline, fat
accumulates in hepatocytes, leading to **nonalcoholic fatty liver disease
(NAFLD)**.

In a controlled trial of 57 healthy adults, **77% of men, 80% of postmenopausal
women, and 44% of premenopausal women** developed fatty liver and/or muscle
damage on a choline-deficient diet. A cross-sectional study of 56,195 Chinese
adults confirmed an inverse relationship between dietary choline intake and
NAFLD risk.

## Cell Membrane Integrity

Choline is the backbone of **phosphatidylcholine** and **sphingomyelin**, the
two dominant phospholipids in every cell membrane in the body. These lipids
maintain membrane fluidity, signal transduction, and cell-to-cell communication.
Choline deficiency disrupts cell proliferation and gene expression, effects that
are especially damaging during fetal development when cells are rapidly
dividing.

## Heart & Vascular Health

Choline's metabolite **betaine** donates a methyl group to convert 
**homocysteine → methionine** via the enzyme BHMT (betaine-homocysteine
methyltransferase). Elevated homocysteine is an independent risk factor for
cardiovascular disease and stroke.

However, there's a nuance: choline is also a precursor to **TMAO (trimethylamine
N-oxide)**, produced by gut bacteria. Some observational studies link higher
TMAO to *increased* cardiovascular risk, so the net cardiovascular effect of
choline is still being studied. Phosphatidylcholine has shown modest
cholesterol-lowering effects by activating lecithin-cholesterol
acyltransferase (LCAT).

## Metabolism, DNA & Gene Expression

Choline serves as a **methyl donor** in one-carbon metabolism, working alongside
folate, vitamin B₁₂, B₆, and riboflavin to generate **S-adenosylmethionine
(SAM)**, the universal methyl donor for DNA synthesis, repair, and gene 
regulation. About 50% of people carry genetic variants (e.g., MTHFR 
polymorphisms) that increase their methylation demand, making them more 
vulnerable to choline inadequacy.

## Recommended Intakes

| Life Stage                     | Adequate Intake (mg/day) |
|:-------------------------------|-------------------------:|
| Adult men (19+)                |                      550 |
| Adult women (19+)              |                      425 |
| Pregnancy                      |                      450 |
| Lactation                      |                      550 |
| Tolerable Upper Limit (adults) |                    3,500 |

## Deficiency Risk Factors

- **Pregnancy & lactation** (highest demand)
- **Vegetarian/vegan diets** (fewer animal-source foods)
- **Postmenopausal status** (estrogen influences choline metabolism)
- **Genetic variants** affecting methylation pathways
- **Total parenteral nutrition (TPN)** without choline supplementation

## Best Dietary Sources (per serving)

| **Food**                 | **Choline (mg)** |
|:-------------------------|-----------------:|
| Beef liver, 3.5 oz       |             ~354 |
| Chicken liver, 3.5 oz    |             ~344 |
| Egg, 1 large (yolk)      |         ~147–169 |
| Salmon, 3.5 oz           |             ~117 |
| Soybeans, ½ cup cooked   |         ~100–120 |
| Poultry (breast), 3.5 oz |           ~75–85 |
| Tofu, ½ cup              |           ~45–55 |
| Peanut butter, 2 tbsp    |           ~25–30 |
| Broccoli, 1 cup cooked   |              ~32 |

For most people, **2 or 3 eggs plus a serving of meat or fish** covers the
daily requirement. Vegans and vegetarians may need to lean heavily on soy,
legumes, and fortified foods, or consider a supplement (common forms: **choline
bitartrate**, **phosphatidylcholine**, **citicoline**).
